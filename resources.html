Understanding mental health

Clear, kind information for everyone in Rwanda, in plain words.

What is mental health?

Mental health is how we think, feel and cope with everyday life. Everyone has it, just like physical health, and it can change over time. Looking after it is not a luxury. It helps us study, work, care for our families and enjoy life.

Signs it may be time to talk to someone

These are not diagnoses. If something feels wrong for more than a couple of weeks, or it is getting in the way of daily life, it is worth talking to someone.

Stress

  • Trouble sleeping
  • Headaches or a tight body
  • Feeling overwhelmed by school or work

Constant worry

  • Worrying that is hard to switch off
  • A racing heart or restlessness
  • Avoiding places or people

Low mood

  • Sadness or emptiness for weeks
  • Losing interest in things you enjoyed
  • No energy, or changes in appetite

Grief, trauma, and alcohol or drug use can also affect mental health. Help is available for these too.

Myths and facts

“Mental illness is a weakness or a punishment.”

Mental health conditions are health conditions. They can affect anyone, and they are not caused by weakness, a curse or something a person did wrong. Many people recover or learn to manage them well with support.

“Only people who are very ill need a therapist.”

Therapy is also for everyday struggles such as exam stress, grief, family problems or feeling stuck. Talking early can stop problems from growing.

“Talking about it makes it worse.”

For most people, talking to someone they trust, or to a professional, brings relief and makes them feel less alone.

“People with mental illness are dangerous.”

Most people with mental health conditions are not violent. They are far more likely to suffer in silence, and that is why kindness and understanding matter.

“You should handle it on your own.”

Asking for help is a sign of strength. Nobody has to carry a heavy burden alone.

How to support someone you care about

Listen without judging

Give them your time. You don’t need to fix everything. Being heard already helps.

Ask and encourage

Ask how they really are. Gently encourage them to talk to a counsellor or doctor, and offer to help them find one.

Keep checking in

A short message a few days later shows you still care. Look after yourself too.

Small habits that help

These support wellbeing. They do not replace treatment when someone needs it.

Rest

Try to sleep at regular times and take real breaks from work or study.

Move and eat well

A daily walk and regular meals help both body and mind.

Stay connected

Spend time with people you trust. Cut back on alcohol and drugs, which often make things worse.

If you or someone you know is in danger

If someone talks about ending their life, or you are afraid for their safety, take it seriously. Stay with them and get help right away. Call the MindCare toll-free line, go to the nearest health centre or district hospital, or Ndera Neuropsychiatric Hospital, or call your local emergency number. Please do not leave the person alone.
